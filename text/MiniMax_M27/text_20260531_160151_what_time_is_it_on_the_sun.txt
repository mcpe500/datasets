**Short answer:**  
There isn’t a single “clock time” for the Sun the way there is for Earth.  The Sun is a ball of plasma that rotates, so any “time” on the Sun is defined by its rotation rather than by a 24‑hour day/night cycle.

---

### How the Sun tells “time”

| What we mean | What it means on the Sun |
|--------------|--------------------------|
| **Earth‑like day** | The Sun’s *synodic* rotation period – the time it takes the same surface point to face Earth again – is **≈ 27 Earth days** (the equatorial sidereal rotation is about 25 days). |
| **Local “solar noon”** | A point on the Sun experiences solar noon when its heliographic longitude line (the line of constant longitude) lines up with the Earth‑Sun line. In solar‑physics terminology this is expressed as the **Carrington longitude** of that point. |
| **Time zones** | The Sun has no fixed surface features, so there’s no “time‑zone” that can be assigned to the whole star.  Different latitudes rotate at different rates (differential rotation), so a “local” solar time would be different for each latitude. |
| **Human clocks** | Spacecraft and scientists usually keep **UTC (Coordinated Universal Time)** for scheduling observations, and they translate events into a solar coordinate system (e.g., Carrington rotation number or heliographic longitude).  The Sun itself does not run on a 24‑hour clock. |

### In everyday terms

*If you stood on the Sun’s visible surface (ignoring the fact that it’s plasma), you would see the Sun rotate around you, completing one full turn in about **27 Earth days**.  That’s a “solar day.”  

If you tried to set a clock that ticked once per solar second (i.e., 1⁄86400 of a solar day), a solar second would be about **0.032 seconds** of Earth time – not very useful for human activity!*

### How scientists reference “solar time”

1. **Carrington Rotation** – a numbering system that starts at a specific longitude (the “Carrington meridian”) each time the Sun makes a full rotation as seen from Earth.  
2. **Heliographic Coordinates** – latitude and longitude measured from the Sun’s equator and a reference meridian (the Carrington meridian).  The Sun’s “local time” at any point is the longitude measured from that meridian, expressed in days (or fractions of the 27‑day rotation period).  

So when someone asks, “What time is it on the Sun?” the best we can say is something like:  

> “At the moment, the Sun’s central meridian (the line that faces Earth) is at Carrington longitude ≈ X°.  From that meridian, a point 90° further east would be at solar noon after about a quarter of the 27‑day solar rotation, i.e., after roughly **6.75 Earth days**.”

---

#### TL;DR

- The Sun has no 24‑hour clock; its “day” is about **27 Earth days** (synodic rotation).  
- “Solar time” is defined by the Sun’s rotation angle, usually expressed as **Carrington longitude** (or other heliographic coordinate).  
- Scientists keep using **UTC** for scheduling, but the Sun itself doesn’t have a single, universal “time.”
