The Sun doesn’t keep a clock the way Earth does. It’s a ball of hot plasma, and there is no fixed “time zone” or a single “local time” that applies to the whole star.  

### Why there isn’t a simple “time” on the Sun  

| What we use on Earth | What applies to the Sun |
|----------------------|--------------------------|
| 24‑hour day, divided into time zones tied to a planetary surface | No solid surface; the Sun rotates, but different latitudes rotate at different rates. |
| Uniform “solar time” at each longitude (e.g., 12:00 pm is the moment the Sun crosses the meridian) | “Solar time” on the Sun would be defined at a particular point on its visible disk (the photosphere), but the Sun’s visible hemisphere is always illuminated – there is no night. |
| Time zone offsets (e.g., UTC+1, UTC‑5) | No official offset; we instead use the Carrington rotation coordinate system to map heliographic longitude to Earth‑based Universal Time (UT). |

### How we talk about “time” on the Sun  

1. **Rotation period** – The Sun’s equator completes one rotation in roughly **27 Earth days** (sidereal period ≈ 25.38 days as seen from Earth, about 27 days when measured from a fixed point in space). The rotation slows toward the poles (≈ 35 days at 60° latitude).

2. **Carrington rotation (CR)** – Solar physicists number each full rotation of the Sun, starting from a reference epoch (9 Nov 1853 12:00 UT). The current CR can be computed from UT, and each CR lasts ~27 days. Inside a CR you can specify a **heliographic longitude** (e.g., the central meridian’s longitude at a given UT). That longitude tells you the “local solar time” for a point on the Sun’s visible disk.

3. **Local solar time on the Sun** – If you pick a point on the solar surface, its “noon” is the moment that point’s heliographic longitude faces the Earth. Because the Sun rotates, a different longitude faces Earth a few minutes later in Earth time. If you wanted to convert Earth time to “solar hours”, you can use the ratio:
   \[
   \frac{24\ \text{h (Earth)}}{27\ \text{d (Sun)}} = \frac{24}{27\times24}= \frac{1}{27}\ \text{solar hour per Earth hour}
   \]
   In other words, 1 hour of Earth time corresponds to about 0.037 solar‑hour (≈ 2 minutes of solar rotation).

### Bottom line  

- **There is no single “time” that applies everywhere on the Sun.**  
- The Sun rotates, completing a turn roughly every 27 Earth days, and astronomers map that rotation with the Carrington rotation number and heliographic longitude.  
- At any given UT, some parts of the Sun are at “noon” (the side facing Earth) while other parts are at “midnight” (the side facing away).  

If you’re interested in the current heliographic longitude of a particular feature (like a sunspot) or the Carrington rotation number for today’s date, you can compute it from the latest Universal Time using the Carrington rotation formula or consult a solar‑weather service (e.g., NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center). But in the everyday sense of “what time is it on the Sun?” the answer is simply: **the Sun doesn’t have a clock; its day is a 27‑day cycle, and each point has its own local “solar time” as it rotates.**
