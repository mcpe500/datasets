The boiling point of ordinary cow’s milk is only a little higher than that of pure water. At standard atmospheric pressure (1 atm ≈ 101.3 kPa) milk typically begins to boil at about **100.2 °C – 100.5 °C** (≈ 212.4 °F – 212.9 °F).  

**Why it’s higher than water**

- **Dissolved solutes:** Milk contains lactose, proteins, minerals (e.g., calcium, magnesium, potassium), and small amounts of fat. These dissolved substances raise the boiling point—a classic colligative property (similar to how salt water boils at a slightly higher temperature than pure water).  
- **Protein and fat content:** The exact value can vary slightly with the milk’s composition (e.g., whole milk vs. skim). Whole milk, which has more fat, may be at the higher end of the range, while skim milk is closer to water’s 100 °C.

**Effect of pressure**

- At lower pressures (higher altitudes) the boiling point drops, just as it does for water. For example, at ~3,000 m (≈10,000 ft) where atmospheric pressure is about 70 kPa, milk will boil around 90–92 °C.
- At higher-than‑standard pressures (e.g., in a pressure cooker) the boiling point rises, and milk can reach temperatures well above 100 °C before bubbling vigorously.

**Quick reference**

| Condition | Approx. Boiling Point of Milk |
|-----------|------------------------------|
| Sea level (1 atm) | 100.2 °C – 100.5 °C (212.4 °F – 212.9 °F) |
| ~3,000 m altitude | ~90 °C – 92 °C (194 °F – 198 °F) |
| Pressure cooker (≈1.5 atm) | ~105 °C – 108 °C (221 °F – 226 °F) |

So, if you’re heating milk on the stovetop at sea level, expect it to start a rolling boil a few tenths of a degree higher than the 100 °C you’d see with water.
