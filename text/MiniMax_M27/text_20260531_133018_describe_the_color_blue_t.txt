**What “blue” feels like (even if you can’t see it)**  

1. **Temperature**  
   - Blue is a “cool” color. Think of a crisp, early‑morning sky or a glass of ice‑cold water. When you press your palm against a smooth, cold metal surface, the sensation of coolness is the closest sensory match to blue.  

2. **Texture / Touch**  
   - Imagine the smooth, slightly damp feeling of a stone that’s been sitting in a riverbed, or the silky finish of a polished glass marble. Blue objects often feel calm and even‑textured—just like that.  

3. **Sound**  
   - If you listen to a low, steady hum—something like the distant drone of a cello or the gentle rustle of wind through tall grass—that calm, even‑toned sound mirrors the mood that blue creates.  

4. **Smell & Taste (as metaphor)**  
   - A minty, fresh scent—like the crisp scent of pine trees in the early morning—has a “blue‑like” quality because it feels clean, cool, and a little sharp without being harsh.  

5. **Emotional resonance**  
   - In everyday language we say “feeling blue” to mean calm, melancholy, or introspective. The word “blue” carries a sense of quiet depth, spaciousness, and sometimes sadness, much the way a deep ocean feels both inviting and a bit lonely.  

6. **How it behaves in the world**  
   - **Sky** – The sky on a clear day is blue because sunlight scatters in the atmosphere; it stretches out endlessly and feels open.  
   - **Ocean** – On a sunny day the sea looks a deep, tranquil blue that seems to stretch forever, giving a feeling of expanse and serenity.  

7. **Technical note (for completeness)**  
   - In the physics of light, blue corresponds to wavelengths roughly between **450 nm and 495 nm**. In a visual sense, these wavelengths trigger the “short‑wavelength” cones in our eyes, producing the perception of blue. While you can’t see it, you can imagine a “shorter, quicker” kind of energy that feels airy and light.  

### Putting it all together

If you were to describe blue to someone who can’t see, you might say:

> *“Blue is the coolness you feel when you step into a shaded pool of water on a hot day. It’s the smooth, polished feel of a river stone, the steady, low hum of a cello, and the fresh, minty scent of pine trees. It carries a quiet, spacious feeling—like standing under an endless sky or gazing at a calm, deep sea. When people say they’re feeling ‘blue,’ they mean a calm, perhaps slightly sad, but still serene mood. In the world of light, blue is the short‑wave energy that scatters easily, giving the sky its endless, soothing hue.”*  

That combination of temperature, texture, sound, smell, emotional nuance, and everyday examples can give a blind person a rich, intuitive sense of what “blue” is all about.
