**Short answer:** There isn’t a “thing” waiting at a rainbow’s end—there’s only the way the light happens to be bent and reflected for *your* particular point of view.  

Below is a quick tour of why the rainbow seems to have a terminus, what folk‑storytelling says you’d find there, and what’s really happening in the physics of the phenomenon.

---

## 1. The folk‑lore answer – the pot of gold

*In Irish legend and many children’s tales the “end” of a rainbow hides a **leprechaun’s pot of gold**, guarded so that no one can claim it.*  

While it’s a charming image, the story was never meant to be taken literally. It was a way for oral tradition to embed a moral (don’t chase fleeting riches) rather than to describe a physical location.

---

## 2. The scientific reality – it’s an optical illusion, not an object

### 2.1 How a rainbow is created  

| Step | What happens | Why it matters |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| **Sunlight** | Parallel rays strike millions of raindrops. | Provides the “white” light that will be split. |
| **Refraction (first)** | Light bends when moving from air into water; the amount depends on wavelength (color). | This separates the colors (violet bends most, red least). |
| **Internal reflection** | Light reflects off the back of the drop. | Keeps the colors inside the drop for the next step. |
| **Refraction (second)** | Light exits the drop, bending again. | Produces the final cone of colored light that reaches your eye. |
| **Geometric condition** | For a given drop, the emergent rays are most intense at about **42°** from the line opposite the sun (the “antisolar line”). | This angle creates the characteristic circular arc you see. |

### 2.2 Why there’s no “end”

1. **You’re always at the tip of a cone**  
   - The rainbow you see is the set of all raindrops that lie on a *cone* of ~42° around the antisolar line.  
   - The tip of that cone is **your eye**. So the “end” of the rainbow is wherever you stand.

2. **It moves with you**  
   - Walk forward and the rainbow recedes; the angle to the antisolar point stays the same, so the cone tip follows you.  
   - No matter how fast you run, you can’t catch up.

3. **Only half of a circle is visible from the ground**  
   - From ground level the lower half of the circle is usually hidden by the horizon.  
   - From an aircraft (or a high hill) you can see the *full circle*—the “end” becomes the opposite side of the ring.

4. **It’s a distribution of angles, not a physical object**  
   - The rainbow has no mass, no surface, and no “endpoint” you could touch.  
   - What you see is a *pattern* of light intensity versus direction.

### 2.3 What you could *actually* find if you walked there

| Situation | What you’d encounter |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **Walking toward the apparent arc** | More raindrops that satisfy the ~42° angle for your new position → the rainbow slides ahead of you. |
| **Reaching the horizon where the arc would “touch” ground** | Typically, the rainbow ends when the rain stops or the sun moves too low. If rain continues, you simply see a lower, dimmer portion of the circle. |
| **From a plane looking straight down** | A full circular rainbow; the “end” is the opposite side of the circle, but again it’s just another set of raindrops at the same angle. |

---

## 3. Varieties of rainbows—different “ends”

| Type | Appearance | Reason |
|------|------------|--------|
| **Primary rainbow** | Single arc, colors from red (outer) to violet (inner). | One internal reflection per drop. |
| **Double rainbow** | Second, fainter arc appears outside the primary, with colors reversed. | Two internal reflections per drop. |
| **Fogbow** | Wide, whitish arc with faint colors, often seen in mist. | Droplets are tiny; diffraction blurs the colors. |
| **Moonbow** | Pale, silvery arc at night caused by moonlight. | Same physics as a sun‑lit rainbow, but much weaker. |
| **Supernumerary rainbow** | Extra, faint bands inside the primary arc. | Caused by interference of light waves in very uniform droplets. |

Each of these follows the same angular rule (≈40–42°), so none of them have a solid endpoint either.

---

## 4. Quick “thought experiment” to illustrate

1. **Imagine a flashlight** shining through a glass bead.  
2. Hold the bead at a fixed distance (≈10 cm) from a wall and tilt it until you see a bright colored spot on the wall.  
3. Move the bead closer to the wall → the colored spot gets larger, but you can never *reach* the spot with the bead; you keep needing to adjust the angle.

That’s exactly how a rainbow behaves: the “spot” is an angular region, not a place you can touch.

---

## 5. TL;DR – the punch line

- **Myth**: A leprechaun’s pot of gold.  
- **Reality**: A rainbow is a cone of light at ~42° from the antisolar direction, and you’re always at its tip. There’s no physical “end” to catch, only the illusion of one that moves with you.  

So if you ever chase a rainbow’s end, you’ll find the same thing each time you get there: **you, standing somewhere else, looking at light refracted by raindrops**.
